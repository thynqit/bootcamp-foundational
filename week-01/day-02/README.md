# Day 2 – Software Development Lifecycle (SDLC)

> Part of the Thynqit Labs – Foundational Engineering Bootcamp

---

## 📌 Overview

The Software Development Lifecycle (SDLC) defines the structured process used to build, test, and deliver software systems.

It provides a framework for:

- planning engineering work
- managing risk
- ensuring software quality
- delivering value incrementally

Without a defined lifecycle, software projects often suffer from:

- scope creep
- unclear ownership
- poor quality
- missed deadlines
- unstable releases

Understanding SDLC helps engineers work effectively in professional engineering environments.

## 🧠 Core Concepts

### 1. What is SDLC?

SDLC is a structured process used by engineering teams to build software systems from idea to deployment and ongoing maintenance.

Typical phases include:

1. Requirement Gathering  
2. System Design  
3. Development  
4. Testing  
5. Deployment  
6. Maintenance  

These phases help teams organize work and ensure that systems are built in a controlled and predictable way.

Depending on the development model, these phases may occur **sequentially or iteratively**.

---

### 2. Waterfall Model

The Waterfall model is a **linear and sequential approach** to software development.

Each phase must be completed before moving to the next.

Characteristics:

- requirements are defined upfront
- documentation is extensive
- feedback cycles occur late in development
- changes are difficult after development begins

Waterfall works best when:

- requirements are stable
- system scope is clearly defined
- changes are unlikely

However, it can introduce risk when requirements evolve over time.

---

### 3. Agile Model

Agile development emphasizes:

- iterative development
- continuous feedback
- incremental delivery
- collaboration with stakeholders

Instead of building the entire system at once, Agile teams deliver software in **small working increments**.

Benefits include:

- faster feedback
- reduced risk
- improved adaptability
- closer collaboration with stakeholders

Most modern engineering teams use **Agile-inspired development processes**.

---

### 4. Iterative vs Incremental Development

Two key ideas often appear in modern development approaches.

**Iterative Development**

A feature or system is improved repeatedly through multiple cycles.

Example: improving a recommendation algorithm across several releases.

**Incremental Development**

Small working pieces of the system are delivered step by step.

Example: launching an e-commerce system with basic checkout, then later adding reviews and recommendations.

Most modern teams combine **iterative and incremental development**.

---

### 5. Why SDLC Matters

Without a structured lifecycle:

- deadlines become unpredictable
- teams become misaligned
- quality decreases
- system complexity increases
- production defects rise

SDLC introduces **discipline, coordination, and predictability** into the engineering process.

It helps teams build complex systems in a manageable way.

---

## 🌍 Real-World Relevance

In real engineering environments:

- poor requirement gathering leads to expensive rework
- lack of structured testing introduces critical bugs
- unclear deployment processes cause outages
- misaligned teams deliver the wrong features

Many large-scale failures occur because of **process breakdowns rather than coding mistakes**.

Understanding SDLC helps engineers avoid these risks.

---

## 🧩 Practical Understanding

Imagine your team is building an e-commerce platform.

Consider the following questions:

- How will requirements be gathered and prioritized?
- When will system architecture and database schemas be designed?
- Who is responsible for testing the system?
- How will software be deployed safely?
- How will production issues be handled?

Thinking through these questions helps engineers understand why structured processes are necessary.

---

## ⚠️ Common Mistakes

Common misunderstandings about SDLC include:

- believing SDLC only applies to large companies
- assuming Agile means no planning
- skipping documentation entirely
- treating testing as optional

Even small teams benefit from structured development processes.
